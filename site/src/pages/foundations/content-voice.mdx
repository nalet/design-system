# content & voice

The words on a screen do as much work as its layout. They say what is true and what people can do about it, in the person's language, not the system's. So we keep them short, plain and true.

This page is about how the words sound. Where they come from, and what they leave out, is on [meaningful text](/design-system/foundations/meaningful-text/).

## the voice

The system speaks with two voices, and the typeface tells them apart.

- **<Term id="monospace">Monospace</Term> is the system's voice, and it is lowercase.** Titles and headings; buttons, tabs and navigation; statuses and badges; table and column headers; menu items, toolbar and filter labels; numbers and codes. It is short and easy to scan, and the lowercase is part of the identity, in every language. Names and codes inside it keep their own capitals: a series called “The Quiet Harbour” stays “The Quiet Harbour” in a group label, a language stays “German” in a button that reads “find German for 4 selected”, an episode stays “S04E07”, and an id stays as it is. The components lowercase this <Term id="chrome">chrome</Term>, and only this, so a name inside a lowercase label, such as a button or a dialog title, is marked as a name and keeps its case.
- **The <Term id="body-face">body face</Term> is the reader's language, and it follows that language's rules.** It carries everything that explains: descriptions and subtitles; the messages in banners, toasts and dialogs; the descriptions of empty states; field labels, hints and errors; checkbox and switch labels; and the text of these guidelines. It is written in <Term id="sentence-case">sentence case</Term>: a capital at the start of each sentence, for names, for “I”, for abbreviations such as HEVC, GB, WCAG, CSS or URL, and for German nouns. Nothing lowercases it.
- **Why two voices.** The brand lives in the monospace layer, where text is short and set apart. Easy reading, and correct spelling in every language, live in the layer people actually read.
- **Short.** A label is two or three words, a message one or two sentences. Leave out adverbs, “please” and “successfully”.
- **Plain.** No emoji, no exclamation marks, no marketing words, and no jokes in error messages.
- **Direct.** Speak to the reader (“pick a release”), say who does what when it matters (“the catalog holds them without an identity”), and use the active voice.

## write what is true, then what to do

Most of what a screen says has one of five shapes. Each one says what is true first, and then what people can do.

| part | says | for example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A <Term id="status">status</Term> | The state, in the object's own words | “on its way”, “needs attention”, “connection firewalled” |
| Its detail | Why, or what happens next | “nothing found · again in 6h”, “the release was removed by its source” |
| A message | What is true, and what it means | “105 titles can't be checked: the catalog holds them without an identity.” |
| An action | A verb and its object | “ask the catalog again”, “grab 8 selected”, “clear filters” |
| An empty state | What is true, why, and the way forward | “no matches” · “Nothing in this list fits the search and filters.” · “clear filters” |

## primary text stays human

Ids, codes, raw errors and technical status codes help an operator fix things, so we keep them, just not in the sentence. A <C slug="banner" /> puts them under its “details”; a table puts them behind a details toggle on each row; a status cell shows a raw search error when the pointer rests on it.

The main text is what a person can act on without knowing how the system works inside.

## buttons start with a verb

- “save changes”, “grab 8 selected”, “ask the catalog again”, “pick a release”.
- Name the object when the screen holds more than one kind of thing, and put the count in an action for several items.
- Avoid “ok”, “submit”, “yes”, “click here” and “learn more”: say what will happen.
- In a dialog, the confirming button repeats the verb of the title (“delete volume” confirms with “delete volume”), and the way out is “cancel”.

## numbers and dates

- Counts use a <Term id="thousands-separator">thousands separator</Term>: “2,500”, not “2500”. The components format their counts this way for you.
- A date is said both ways, as a date and as how long ago: “12 oct 2026 · 5d ago”, as the <C slug="table-cells">date cell</C> does.
- “n of m” reads as shown of total: “100 of 2,500”.
- Plurals agree with their number: “1 title”, “105 titles”; “8 episodes queued”, “1 movie queued”.

### in the page's language

The examples on this page are English. On a page in another language, every number, every date and every word follows that language, the components' own words included.

| what | on an English page | on a German page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A count | 2,500 | 2.500 |
| A number with its unit | 12.4 GB | 12,4 GB |
| A date, and how long ago | 12 oct 2026 · 5d ago | 12. okt. 2026 · vor 5 tagen |
| Shown of total | 100 of 2,500 | 100 von 2.500 |
| A sentence that counts | 1 result, 105 results | 1 Ergebnis, 105 Ergebnisse |

- **The page says its language once, and everything on it follows.** A component doesn't choose a language for itself, and a screen doesn't set one cell by cell. A count written one way beside a date written another reads as a mistake.
- **The forms are the language's own.** The separators, the order of a date and the names of the months come from the browser, which knows them for every language. They are never put together by hand. A page that names a region as well gets that region's forms: a count on a Swiss German page reads “2'500”.
- **A sentence that counts is written once for each form the language has.** English has two, “1 result” and “105 results”. Other languages have one, or more than two. The sentence is whole in each form, never a number joined to a word.
- **The two voices hold in every language.** Monospace chrome is lowercase, so a month reads “okt.”. A sentence in Inter follows the language's own rules for capitals, so a German noun keeps its capital.
- **The components' own words belong to the page.** A few components bring a word or two of their own, such as “loading”, “close”, “no rows” and “4 selected”. A page in another language replaces all of them in one place, for the whole page.
- **Not yet: a language written from right to left.** It turns every layout round, which the components don't do.

Planned. Today the components write their counts the English way on every page, and say how long ago in English. The [specs](#specs) list what a page can already change.

## words that mean one thing

Some words come up on every screen. Each of them means one thing, everywhere.

| use | for | not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “missing” | Wanted and not held | “absent”, “lacking” |
| “on its way” | Queued, grabbed, downloading, or waiting to be listed | “in progress” for things that aren't |
| “needs attention” | Failed or blocked: a person has to act | “error” on its own, “problem” |
| “can't be checked” | The system can't know | “unknown” with no reason |
| “held” | In the library | “done” |
| “Portuguese (Brazil)” | A language with its region or script in brackets after it, so that the variants of a language sort together: “Chinese (Simplified)”, “English (New Zealand)” | “Brazilian Portuguese”, “pt-BR” |

## specs

- **Lowercase chrome.** The components set `text-transform: lowercase` on their monospace chrome, and only there. The text in the DOM stays as written.
- **Names inside lowercase chrome.** Wrap the name in `KeepCase`, or give its element the class `nc-keep-case`: `find <KeepCase>German</KeepCase> for 4 selected`. It sets `text-transform: none` on the name alone, in any label that takes elements: buttons, dialog and empty-state titles, tabs, menu items, toolbar headings and statuses.
- **Counts.** `formatCount(2500)` returns “2,500”; it is the formatter the components use. Today: it takes no language, and writes the English way on every page.
- **Dates.** The `DateCell` writes a date both ways. Today: the date follows the cell's `locale` prop, British English unless you set it. How long ago, and “no date” for a screen reader, are English alone.
- **The components' own words.** Today: most are props with an English default, set one component at a time: the spinner's `label`, the banner's `detailsLabel` and `dismissLabel`, the toolbar's `overflowLabel`, `clearLabel` and `formatSelected`, the filter bar's `label`, the multi select's `emptyText`, the table's `empty`, the data table's `formatGrowing`, and the portal's `searchLabel` and `searchPlaceholder`. A few are fixed in English: the dialog's “close”, the data table's “select every visible row”, “select” before a row's name and “show 50 more”, and a tile's “status:”.
- **The page's language.** Planned: the components read the language the page declares, the `lang` of its root element, and format counts, dates and how long ago with the browser's `Intl`. Their own words come from one set per language, given once for the whole page; English is built in.
- **Details.** A banner's `details` prop; a native `<details>` per table row; a raw search error kept in a status cell's hover text.
