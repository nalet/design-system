# app navigation

Most <Term id="app">apps</Term> have more than one screen: a list of things to look at, a way to search, some settings. People need a simple way to move between these <Term id="view">views</Term>, and to always know where they are.

We list an app's views down the left side, in a <Term id="side-navigation">side navigation</Term>. The views are grouped by what people do there, and the view the app opens on comes first.

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <AppWithSideNav />
  <figcaption>A downloads app: its views on the left, grouped into work, find and setup. It opens on the things that need attention.</figcaption>
</figure>

The side navigation and the app layout around it are planned components; the drawings on this page show the idea.

## why down the side

A row of <Term id="tabs">tabs</Term> works well for a handful of views. But apps grow: a downloads app soon has requests, a missing list, downloads, search, sources, clients and settings. In one row, they no longer fit, and daily work gets mixed up with setup.

A list down the side has room to grow. It can be grouped, it can show a number next to a view that needs attention, and it stays in place while the content scrolls.

## when tabs are the better fit

Tabs are still the right choice in two places. They switch between parts of one thing, like the overview, episodes, files and history of a series. And they filter one list, like the counted tabs at the top of a [worklist](/design-system/patterns/worklist/).

A simple way to tell them apart: tabs change what you are looking at, the side navigation changes what you are doing.

## anatomy

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <SideNavAnatomy />
  <figcaption>The side navigation, close up.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **Groups.** Every app uses the same three, in the same order: work for the views people use every day, with the exceptions first; find for search and discovery; setup for sources, clients and settings, at the bottom and only for people who may change them.
2. **Views.** Each has an icon, a name, and, when it helps, a number someone acts on. The number turns to a warning colour only when it means attention.
3. **The current view.** It is marked by the blue bar on its edge and slightly heavier text, so it doesn't rely on colour alone.

## how it behaves

- **The app opens on its first view**, which shows what needs attention (see [principle 1](/design-system/foundations/principles/#1-the-system-does-the-work-people-handle-the-exceptions)), not on its longest list.
- **Setup stays separate from daily work.** It sits in the setup group, and the shell's settings list each app's setup and link straight to it. See [settings and setup](/design-system/patterns/settings-and-setup/).
- **Every view has its own address**, so a tile, a link or the back button can bring people right back to it.
- **On medium-sized screens** the list shrinks to its icons.
- **On a phone** the views move into a menu that opens from the app's name, instead of a row of tabs that has to be scrolled sideways.

## naming

- **Groups** are always called work, find and setup, in every app, so people learn them once.
- **Views** are named the way people talk about them: “needs attention”, “on its way”, “search sources”. The view's title uses the same words.
- **Numbers** only appear when someone acts on them, and use a thousands separator (2,500).

For a downloads app, that gives:

- **Work:** needs attention, on its way, requests, missing, series
- **Find:** search, discover
- **Setup:** search sources, clients, settings
