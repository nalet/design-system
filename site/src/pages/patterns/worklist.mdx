# worklist

A <Term id="worklist">worklist</Term> is a page for working through a list of things that need doing: episodes that are missing, jobs that failed, hosts that don't answer. It shows what needs a person first, and offers one next step for each item.

It is a <Term id="page-layout">page layout</Term>: a whole page, put together from components, so that every list of work looks and works the same, and people only learn it once.

  <WorklistOverview />
  <figcaption>A worklist of missing episodes: counted tabs at the top, one message about the list as a whole, the filters, the selection in the toolbar, and the episodes grouped by season.</figcaption>
</figure>

The live version is on the worklist's [React page](/design-system/react/?path=/docs/patterns-worklist--docs), with 2,644 made-up items: select rows and grab them, use a row's menu, collapse a season, filter and search.

## when it fits

Use a worklist when people work through many items that each need an action or a decision, when those items fall into a few states that people sort by, like “needs attention” first, and when people often act on several items at once.

For other jobs, other pages fit better:

- **One item in depth.** A page for that item (the detail page is planned), or a <C slug="card" /> for a summary.
- **A few numbers to keep an eye on**, with no list to work through. <Term id="number-tile">Number tiles</Term> on the [home page](/design-system/patterns/home-and-tiles/).
- **Reference information nobody acts on.** A plain <C slug="table" />.
- **Settings.** A form; see [forms and settings](/design-system/patterns/forms-and-settings/).
- **A dozen items without states.** A <C slug="data-table" /> on its own, without tabs or filters.

## anatomy

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <WorklistAnatomy />
  <figcaption>The worklist, from top to bottom.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **Header.** The list's name, like “missing”, a line that says which items belong in it, and a quiet line of facts, such as when the list was last refreshed.
2. **Counted tabs.** The list's states, each with its count: everything first, then the states people sort by. Choosing a tab filters the list. A count takes a colour only when it means something: blue for what is on its way, red for what needs attention, amber for what can't be checked. Plain counts stay grey. These are the <C slug="filter-tabs" />.
3. **A <Term id="banner">banner</Term>.** At most one or two, about the list as a whole: what it leaves out, or a load that failed. See the <C slug="banner" />.
4. **Search and filters**, with the number of items left and a way to clear them all: the <C slug="filter-bar" />.
5. **The toolbar.** The count of what is shown; once rows are selected, the selection and its one <Term id="emphasized-action">emphasized action</Term>. See the <C slug="toolbar">selection toolbar</C>.
6. **The table.** Grouped by the items' parent, like a season of a series, a hundred rows at a time. People can sort it and select rows. See the <C slug="data-table" />.
7. **A menu on every row**, with that row's actions, instead of buttons: the <C slug="action-menu" />.

Two more parts appear only at times. When there is nothing to list, a message takes the table's place and says which kind of empty it is. And after an action, a short <Term id="toast">toast</Term> says what happened, like “8 episodes queued”; when an action fails, a banner says so instead.

## how it behaves

- **Tabs first, then filters.** The tab decides which items are in play; search and filters narrow it down from there. The counts come from the same data as the table, so a count and its list always agree.
- **A tab can count items the list doesn't hold**, like “not yet aired”. Choosing it shows a message that says why there is no list.
- **Changing the view starts again at the top.** A new tab or a new filter goes back to the first hundred rows. A refresh of the data keeps people's place.
- **People act on what they can see.** In the live example, a filter that hides a selected row also unselects it, so “grab 8 selected” never grabs rows that are out of sight. How a selection should behave across filters is [under review](/design-system/patterns/selection-and-bulk-actions/#keeping-a-selection).
- **The toolbar and the table's header stay in view** while the list scrolls.
- **After an action**, the toast counts what happened, “8 episodes queued · 2 skipped: held already or under way”, and the rows update when the data reloads. A failed action shows as an error banner, with the technical details behind “details”.

## when there is nothing to list

When a worklist has nothing to show, the header, the tabs and the banner stay where they are, and the table's place holds a message that says which kind of empty it is: good news, or something nobody can know yet.

## keyboard and screen readers

- The worklist is a section named after its title. Inside an app, the app owns the page's main heading, so the worklist's title is a level below it.
- The tabs are read as tabs, and the list below them as their content.
- The Tab key stops only a few times: once for the tabs, then the banner's buttons, the filters, once for the toolbar, the sort buttons in the table's header, once for all the table's rows, and “show more”.
- Give the table a name and each row a label, so every checkbox and every menu says what it acts on: “select The Quiet Harbour S04E07”, “actions for The Quiet Harbour S04E07”.

## the words

- **Title.** What is in the list, as a noun: “missing”, “failed jobs”.
- **Subtitle.** The rule for what belongs in the list, in sentence case: “Aired, monitored, and not in the library”.
- **Facts.** What readers may wonder about: when the data was last refreshed, what runs by itself.
- **Tabs.** The items' states in their own words, with counts: “missing”, “on their way”, “needs attention”.
- **Toolbar.** The count and what is shown: “2,500 missing”, “81 need attention”.
- **Groups.** The parent's own name and its count: “The Quiet Harbour · S04 · 8 missing”.
