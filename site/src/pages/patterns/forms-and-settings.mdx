# forms and settings

Settings change how something works: when a backup runs, how many days it keeps, who gets an email. Some settings take effect the moment they are changed. Others are part of a form, and wait until people press save. This page is about telling the two apart, and about how a form helps people get things right.

  <SettingsOverview />
  <figcaption>A settings page: switches that apply at once at the top, and a form below that waits for save.</figcaption>

The live version is on this pattern's [React page](/design-system/react/?path=/docs/patterns-forms-and-settings--docs), together with how it is built.

## when it fits

- For a page or a panel of settings that people change now and then: a schedule, limits, what to include, how things look.
- For any form that ends with a save: creating something, or editing it.
- For an app's own setup views, which are forms like this one; [settings and setup](/design-system/patterns/settings-and-setup/) says where each kind of setting lives.

Other jobs need something else:

- **Filtering or searching a list.** Use the <C slug="filter-bar" />: filters apply as they change, and nothing is saved.
- **A decision that has to come first**, such as confirming a delete. Use a <C slug="modal">dialog</C> with the question and two buttons.
- **One action with nothing to fill in**, like “check now”. Use a <C slug="button" />.
- **Settings that each apply on their own, at once.** They need no form and no save button: a card of <C slug="switch">switches</C>.

## instant or saved

Whether a change applies at once or waits for save is the most important thing to get right on a settings page. People need to know whether they are done.

- **A switch applies the moment it is flipped.** Switches sit outside the form, in a card without a save button. When the change shows somewhere else, or takes a while, a <Term id="toast">toast</Term> confirms it: “Email alerts off”. If it can't be applied, the switch flips back, and an error banner says why.
- **A checkbox is part of the form.** It waits for save, like every other field.
- **Keep the two apart.** A switch above a save button leaves people unsure whether it applied; a checkbox without a save button sends them looking for one.

Today there is no radio button component; a group of the browser's own radio buttons does the job.

## anatomy

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <SettingsAnatomy />
  <figcaption>The settings page, close up.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **Page title**, with a quiet line of facts under it.
2. **Instant settings**: a card of switches, each with its own label and, where the effect isn't obvious, a hint. No footer.
3. **The form**, named after its card's title, so people can jump to it.
4. **Fields**: each with its label, a hint, and the error in place of the hint when there is one. See the <C slug="field" />.
5. **Option groups**: a question that the options answer, with checkboxes under it. For one choice out of two or three, a group of radio buttons; out of more, a <C slug="select" />.
6. **Footer**: the “unsaved changes” signal, “cancel” as a quiet button, and “save changes” as the one <Term id="emphasized-action">emphasized</Term> button, at the end of the row.

Three more parts appear only at times: a success message above the content, after a save that changed what the page shows; an error summary at the top of a long form, after a save that found errors; and a toast that confirms a save.

## numbers and ranges

Some values are numbers: how loud, how far into a film, how many seconds to count down. Two planned inputs take them, the slider and the stepper. Until they are built, a <C slug="select" /> with the values, or an <C slug="input" /> with the number and its unit after it, does the job.

| the value | use |
| --- | --- |
| A value in a range that people judge by its effect: the volume, a place in a film, a brightness | A slider, with the value in words beside it |
| A whole number, a few small steps from end to end: a countdown in seconds, how many copies to keep | A stepper |
| A number from a wide range, or one people know exactly: a port, a size in GB | An input, with its unit after it |
| One of a few named values | A select, or radio buttons |

### the slider

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <SliderKinds />
  <figcaption>Three sliders: how loud, and where you are, in a player; and a value in a range, in settings.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <SliderAnatomy />
  <figcaption>A slider, close up.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **Label**, as a field's, in the body face: “Volume”. In a player, an icon can stand in for it, and the label is then the slider's name for screen readers.
2. **Track**: the whole range, from the lowest value at the start to the highest at the end.
3. **The part up to the thumb**, in blue. On a seek bar, what has loaded can follow it, in a lighter grey.
4. **Thumb**: a square, as every frame is.
5. **The value in words**, after the track: “60%”. On a seek bar, the time played comes before the track, and the length after it.

- **The words say the value.** The thumb shows about where it is, and the words say exactly, so the value never rests on the thumb alone.
- **A press on the track moves the thumb there**, and dragging moves it with the pointer or the finger. The whole track is the target; on a touch screen, it answers in a band 48 high.
- **The arrow keys take one step**, Page Up and Page Down a larger one, and Home and End go to the two ends. The slider is one <Term id="tab-stop">tab stop</Term>. The product sets the size of a step.
- **It applies as it moves** where people hear or see the effect, like the volume: outside a form, a slider is an instant setting, like a switch. Inside a form, it waits for save, like every field.
- **Sizes.** The slider takes the height of the controls in its row, so the row lines up. Its track is 4 high, the first step of the scale, and its thumb is a square of 16, the size of a checkbox's box.

On a TV:

- **A slider takes a row of its own.** In a row with other controls, left and right would move its thumb and never the focus, so people couldn't get past it. On a row of its own, like a player's seek bar above the player's buttons, left and right change the value, and up and down move on.
- **Where it has to share a row**, like a player's volume next to its buttons, up and down change it, and left and right move on.
- **A value people set exactly**, like a countdown, is a stepper or a list on a TV, not a slider. Apple's tvOS has no slider at all.

### the stepper

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <StepperAnatomy />
  <figcaption>A stepper, close up.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **Label**, as a field's: “Countdown”.
2. **Minus**, one step down.
3. **The value with its unit**: “5 s”.
4. **Plus**, one step up.
5. **Hint**: the range, “From 1 to 15 seconds”, or the error in its place.

- **One step a press.** Minus and plus change the value by one step. At either end, the button that would go past it is dimmed and does nothing, and the value stays.
- **For a few steps.** A stepper suits a short range, a few presses from one end to the other. Where people may want a value far away, let them type it: the value is then a field, with minus and plus on either side of it.
- **The keyboard.** The value is the one tab stop. The up and down arrow keys take a step, and Home and End go to the two ends. Minus and plus are for the pointer and for touch, and the Tab key passes them.
- **Instant or saved**, like every field: in a form, it waits for save.
- **Sizes.** The stepper is as high as a field, in one of the three heights. Minus and plus are squares of that height, like icon buttons, and on a touch screen each one answers in 48.

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <StepperStates />
  <figcaption>A stepper at its lowest value, and the same setting on a TV, as one row.</figcaption>
</figure>

On a TV, a stepper is one row: its label, and the value between two small arrows at the row's end. Left and right change the value, and up and down move to the next row. Like a slider, it has a row of its own. Apple's tvOS has no stepper either, so the product draws the row itself.

- **The words.** The value is short, with its unit, “5 s”, and is read out in full, “5 seconds”. The hint says the range: “From 1 to 15 seconds”. A value typed out of range gets an error that says how to fix it: “Use a whole number from 1 to 15”.
- **For screen readers.** The slider is the browser's own range input, so a screen reader says it is a slider, with its label and its value in words: “60%”, “42 minutes of 1 hour 52”. The stepper's value is a spin button with its label, its value and its range; minus and plus are named after the value they change, such as “fewer seconds” and “more seconds”.
- **Where this comes from.** The keys follow the WAI-ARIA authoring practices for a [slider](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/slider/) and a [spin button](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/spinbutton/). Apple's guidelines have no [slider](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sliders) and no [stepper](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/steppers) in tvOS, and suggest a field next to a stepper for values that vary widely. Microsoft's [guidance for remotes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/apps/design/input/gamepad-and-remote-interactions) calls a slider that catches left and right in a row a focus trap; one way out it gives is to stack the controls, so that the slider has a row of its own.

## the footer and unsaved changes

- **One footer, at the end of the form.** Its one emphasized button is “save changes”, with “cancel” as a quiet button before it.
- **The form has unsaved changes when a value differs from the saved one.** Typing a value back to what was saved makes the form clean again.
- **While there are unsaved changes, the footer says so**, next to the buttons: “unsaved changes”.
- **Save stays available**, with or without changes, with or without errors. A greyed-out button is skipped by the Tab key, so keyboard users may never reach it, and it can't say why it is off. A save that can be pressed answers when it is pressed: with no changes, a toast says “No changes to save”; with errors, the errors show.
- **Pressing “cancel” puts every field back** to its saved value and clears the errors, without asking first: nothing that was saved is lost.
- **On a form taller than the screen, the footer stays in view** at the bottom while the form scrolls.

Today nothing warns people who leave a page with unsaved changes; the app does that, with the browser's own leave-page question.

## checking the values

Checking what people entered is called <Term id="validation">validation</Term>, and it happens when they press save.

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <ValidationStory />
  <figcaption>No errors while people type. On save, the errors show at their fields, a summary counts them, and the focus moves to the first.</figcaption>
</figure>

- **Check on save**, not while people type, and not when they leave a field. An error that appears as people pass through a field scolds them for something they haven't finished.
- **Once a field shows an error, check it again on every change**, so the error goes the moment the value is right.
- **Save checks every field**, shows the error at each one that is wrong, and sends nothing.
- **Then the <Term id="focus">focus</Term> moves to the first field that needs a fix.** People hear its name, that it is invalid, and its error, and they start where the problem is.
- **On a long form, also sum up at the top**: an error banner that counts what needs a fix and names it in the labels' words. It follows the fixes, and goes when nothing is left. A long form is one with more than one group, or one that scrolls; a short form only needs the errors at the fields.
- **The save button doesn't turn off** to signal an error.

## when the save fails

- The save itself can fail. Say so in an error banner at the top of the form, with a way forward, “try again”; the technical part goes under details.
- The fields keep what people typed, and the changes stay unsaved.
- Not in a toast: it leaves after five seconds, and the work still isn't saved.

## after a save

- **While the save runs**, the save button shows that it is busy, so a second press can't send the form twice.
- **When it is done**, the fields show the saved values, and “unsaved changes” goes.
- **A toast confirms it**: “Settings saved”. A second save replaces that toast instead of stacking another.
- **When the save changes what the page shows**, such as every time on the page now shown in UTC, say so in a success banner above the content that people can dismiss: “Saved. Times on this page now show in UTC.” The change happened where people weren't looking; the banner says why. No toast as well.

## keyboard and accessibility

| key | what it does |
| --- | --- |
| Tab and Shift+Tab | Moves through the switches, the fields and the options, then cancel and save, in reading order |
| Enter, in a text field | Saves the form: it presses “save changes” |
| Space | Ticks a checkbox, flips a switch, or presses a button |
| Arrow keys | Move through a select's options, or to the next radio button, choosing it |
| Esc | Closes whatever is open inside the form, like a select's list. It doesn't cancel the form |

- The form is named after its card's title, so people can jump to it.
- Only “save changes” sends the form. Enter presses the form's first send button, so a cancel button that sent the form, placed before save, would be the one it pressed.
- Required fields say “required” to <Term id="screen-reader">screen readers</Term>; the browser's own error bubbles stay out of the way.
- A field's error isn't read out when it appears; moving the focus to the first field that needs a fix is what gets it read.
- Error banners are read out as they appear, and again each time their text changes, once per fix. The success banner is read out politely.

Today:

- A success banner that appears with its text already in it may pass in silence on some screen readers. The toast doesn't have that problem.
- The busy save button can't keep the focus while the save runs, so the browser may move the focus to the page. When the save ends, put the focus back on save.
- A toast stays five seconds, and only a pointer resting on it pauses it, not the keyboard. Keep anything people need later out of it: the saved form is the record.
- An error for a checkbox or a switch doesn't mark the box itself. Check option groups as a whole, and say what is wrong in the summary.
- There is no group or radio button component yet. The browser's own, with the question styled like a field's label, does the job.

## the words

- **Title.** What the settings are about: “backup settings”.
- **Labels, hints and errors.** In sentence case, as the <C slug="field" /> page says. A noun for the label, “Retention (days)”; an error that says how to fix it, “Use a whole number from 1 to 90”.
- **An option group's question.** What the options answer: “Back up”, “Notify me about”.
- **Footer.** “save changes” and “cancel”. Not “ok”, “submit” or “apply”.
- **The signal.** “unsaved changes”, and nothing while there are none.
- **The summary.** “Couldn't save.”, then the count and the labels as they are written: “2 settings need a fix: Backup host and Retention (days).”
- **A failed save.** What happened, and that nothing is lost: “Couldn't save the settings. Your changes are still here.” The code goes under details.
- **Toasts.** What happened to what, in sentence case, without a full stop: “Settings saved”, “Email alerts off”, “No changes to save”.
- **The success banner.** “Saved.”, then what changed on the page: “Saved. Times on this page now show in UTC.”
