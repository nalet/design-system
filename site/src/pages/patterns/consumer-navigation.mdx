# consumer navigation

A product for consumers, such as one for watching films, has only a few <Term id="view">views</Term>, and people move between them all the time: home, films, series, their watchlist. They use it on a computer, on a phone held either way, and on a TV across the room. On every one of these screens, the views are the same icons, in the same order: in a <Term id="rail">rail</Term> down the left edge, or, on a phone held upright, in a <Term id="tab-bar">tab bar</Term> at the foot, within reach of the thumb.

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <ConsumerNavOverview />
  <figcaption>One product on four screens, with films as the current view: the same rail of icons down the left edge on a wide screen, on a phone held sideways and on a TV, and a tab bar of icons at the foot of a phone held upright. The phone adds downloads, to watch offline.</figcaption>
</figure>

The rail, the tab bar and the <Term id="top-bar">top bar</Term> are planned components; the drawings on this page show the idea.

## one rail and one tab bar

Two components show the views, one for each shape of screen:

- **The rail**, a column of icons down the left edge, wherever there is room for a column beside the page: on a computer and a tablet, on a phone held sideways, and on a TV.
- **The tab bar**, a row of icons along the foot of a phone held upright, which has no width to spare for a column, where the thumb is.

Both hold the same views, in the same order, as the same icons, and both mark the current view the same way: in the accent blue, with a bar on its edge. People learn the icons on the first screen they use, and find them again on the next. What changes from one screen to the next is what each one has room for:

| screen | the views | their names | settings | the current view |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A computer or a tablet | The rail, under the product's mark in the top bar | A tooltip under the pointer, and for a screen reader | At the rail's foot | Blue on a lighter square, with a bar on its left edge |
| A phone held upright | The tab bar, at the foot | For a screen reader | In the user menu | Blue, with a bar on its top edge |
| A phone held sideways | The rail, beside a slimmer top bar | For a screen reader | In the user menu | Blue, with a bar on its left edge |
| A TV | The rail, which holds the whole frame, because a TV has no top bar | Beside the icons while the focus is in the rail, and for a screen reader | At the rail's foot, with the notifications | Blue, with a bar on its left edge |

- **The lighter square belongs to the wide screen.** There, a view's square lightens while the pointer rests on it, as a quiet button does, and the current view keeps that square. A phone has no pointer, and on a TV a lighter surface means focus ([TV focus](/design-system/patterns/tv-focus/#the-focus-style)), so there the current view has its blue and its bar alone.
- **Settings sit in the rail where it has room for them**: at its foot, apart from the views, on a wide screen and on a TV. On a phone, held either way, they are only in the <Term id="user-menu">user menu</Term>: the tab bar has no room for a sixth tab, and turning the phone should move nothing but the tabs. The user menu keeps settings on every screen, as the top bar has it, so people find them in the same place in every product.

## when it applies

This page is for a product people use for themselves, at home or on the go, with up to five views on one level. An <Term id="app">app</Term> inside the <Term id="portal">portal</Term>, whose views come in groups and grow with its work, keeps its <Term id="side-navigation">side navigation</Term> and its phone menu; see [app navigation](/design-system/patterns/app-navigation/).

The three navigation pages fit together like this:

- **The [top bar](/design-system/patterns/top-bar/)** is on every screen, and belongs to the <Term id="shell">shell</Term>: the product's name, the one search, the notifications and the user menu. Over a rail, the product's mark stands for its name.
- **[App navigation](/design-system/patterns/app-navigation/)** lists an app's views down the side, under the top bar, with their names, in groups.
- **This page** keeps a product's few views out of the top bar: icons alone, in a rail down the left edge, or in a tab bar at the foot of a phone.

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <TwoKinds />
  <figcaption>The same top bar, with the search, the bell and the user menu in the same places, over two kinds of navigation. An app inside the portal lists its views down the side, with their names, in groups. A product for consumers shows its few views as icons in a rail, under its mark, with settings at the rail's foot.</figcaption>
</figure>

| | an app inside the portal | a product for consumers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Its views | As many as its work needs, in the groups work, find and setup | Up to five, on one level, without groups |
| On a wide screen | A side navigation under the top bar, each view with its icon and its name | A rail under the top bar, each view its icon alone, with its name as a tooltip |
| On a medium-sized screen | The side navigation shrinks to its icons | The same rail |
| On a phone | A menu that opens from the app's name | A tab bar of icons at the foot; held sideways, the rail |
| On a TV | — | The rail, which holds the whole frame and shows the names when the focus is in it |
| The start of the top bar | The product's name, then the app's name | The product's mark, as wide as the rail under it |
| Settings | The setup group, at the bottom of the side navigation | At the rail's foot on a wide screen and on a TV, and in the user menu everywhere |

A product whose views need groups, or more than five of them, takes the side navigation instead. Five views fit across a phone, each one press away; a sixth squeezes the tabs, or pushes one out of sight.

A tab bar is not a row of <C slug="tabs" />: tabs switch between the parts of one thing, on one screen, and the tab bar moves between the product's views.

## where it differs from the top bar and app navigation

Until this page is accepted, the [top bar](/design-system/patterns/top-bar/) page says otherwise in three places:

- **Its rule for a product for consumers** ([the same everywhere](/design-system/patterns/top-bar/#the-same-everywhere)) says that “a product for consumers can keep its own main navigation in the bar, such as home, films and series”, and draws them as words between the product's name and the bell. This page takes the views out of the bar. On a wide screen they are icons in a rail, and the bar holds only what the shell puts there: the search, the notifications and the user menu.
- **Its first part** is the product's name, led by the chevron. Over a rail, it is the product's mark, the chevron and the first letter of the name, in a cell as wide as the rail. It still leads home, and it is still named after the product.
- **Its user menu** holds settings, and still does: a product with a rail also puts them at the rail's foot on a wide screen and on a TV.

[App navigation](/design-system/patterns/app-navigation/) is for apps, and stays as it is: it says nothing about a product for consumers.

Once this page is accepted, the top bar's line would read: “A product for consumers shows its few views as icons in a rail down the left edge, with its mark over the rail at the start of the bar. The search, the notifications and the user menu stay where the shell puts them, and look the way the shell draws them; see consumer navigation.” Its drawing of a bar with home, films and series would show the rail instead, and its user menu would say that a product with a rail keeps settings at the rail's foot. App navigation would send a product for consumers to this page, and its side navigation, where a medium-sized screen shrinks it to its icons, could take the rail's sizes and its tooltips, so the two columns of icons look alike.

## the views

- **Up to five, on one level.** Android's guidance gives a navigation bar three to five views. Apple's warns that tabs that don't fit turn the last one into a “more” list, which makes the rest harder to find. Android's guidance for TVs asks for five or six at most, so that the rail never scrolls.
- **The same views, in the same order, on every screen**, so people find each one where it was last time. A view that only one kind of device offers, such as downloads to watch offline on a phone, comes last.
- **Home comes first**, and the product's mark, or its name on a phone, leads back to it too.
- **Every view has its own address**, so a link, a reload or the back button brings people right back to it, as in [app navigation](/design-system/patterns/app-navigation/#how-it-behaves).
- **Search, notifications and the user menu aren't views.** They stay where every screen has them: in the top bar, and on a TV at the top and the foot of the rail. None of them becomes a tab.
- **Settings isn't one of the five.** It has a place of its own: at the rail's foot, where its gear is the current one while settings is open, or in the user menu on a phone, where no tab is current while it is open.

## icons alone

The rail and the tab bar show the views' icons, and no names beside or under them. Names would make the rail wider and the tab bar taller, on every screen, and with a few views, always in the same place, people learn the icons once and then reach for them without looking. Both phone platforms' own guidance shows a name with the tabs, Apple's under every icon and Android's at least under the current one, so icons alone are a choice. They work only when:

- **Every icon is plain and familiar.** A house for home, a film strip for films, a television for series, a bookmark for the watchlist, an arrow into a tray for downloads: people name each one with the view's own word, without being told.
- **It is the same icon in every product**, for the same view, and it means nothing else anywhere. A house is always home. The table below lists them.
- **Each view keeps its name for the people who need it.** The view's name is its link's <Term id="accessible-name">accessible name</Term>, so a <Term id="screen-reader">screen reader</Term> says “films”, and people who use <Term id="speech-input">speech input</Term> say the word the icon makes them think of, and the link answers to it. On a wide screen, the browser also shows the name as its own tooltip while the pointer rests on the icon, as it does with an <C slug="icon-button" />'s label.
- **The current view is marked twice**, by its colour and by its bar.

People who see the screen but use a keyboard or a touch screen never see that tooltip, as with an icon button. That is one more reason for icons nobody has to guess.

An icon that people would name in different ways can't stand alone. A sparkle might mean new or for you, and a compass discover or a map. A view without a plain icon doesn't get a place in the rail or the tab bar: it starts from home instead, or it gets a plainer name and an icon to match.

| view | its icon | in the icon set |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home | A house | `house` |
| Films | A film strip | `film` |
| Series | A television | `tv` |
| Watchlist | A bookmark | `bookmark` |
| Downloads | An arrow into a tray | `download` |
| Search, in the bar | A magnifying glass | `search` |
| Notifications, in the bar | A bell | `bell` |
| Settings, at the rail's foot, or in the user menu on a phone | A gear | `settings` |

The icons come from the system's [icon set](/design-system/styles/iconography/#where-the-icons-come-from).

## on a wide screen

On a computer or a tablet, the views run down the left edge in the rail, under the top bar.

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <WideAnatomy />
  <figcaption>A wide screen, close up, with films as the current view and the pointer resting on watchlist.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **The product's mark**, in the bar's first cell, over the rail and as wide as it: the chevron and the first letter of the product's name, as the system's [app icon](/design-system/styles/brand/#the-app-icon) pairs the chevron with the letter n. It leads home.
2. **The rail**: the views, one under the other, in their order, each its icon alone: home, films, series and watchlist. Each one is a link to its view.
3. **The current view**: its icon is the accent blue, on a lighter square, with a blue bar on its left edge. The bar, not only the colour, says which one it is.
4. **A view's name**, while the pointer rests on it: its square lightens, its icon brightens, and the browser shows the name as its own tooltip.
5. **Settings**, a gear, at the rail's foot, apart from the views. While settings is open, the gear is the current one.
6. **The search, the bell and the user menu** stay where the shell puts them, at the end of the bar, and look the way the shell draws them; see [top bar](/design-system/patterns/top-bar/#what-it-holds).

The page takes the rest of the screen, beside the rail and under the bar. The rail and the bar stay in place while the page scrolls.

## on a phone held upright

The bar keeps what every screen needs, and the views move to a tab bar at the foot of the screen, within reach of the thumb.

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <PhoneAnatomy />
  <figcaption>A phone held upright, with films as the current view.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **The bar**: the product's name, the search as an icon, the bell and the user menu, as the [top bar](/design-system/patterns/top-bar/#search) has them on a phone.
2. **The tab bar**: one tab per view, each one its icon alone, each an equal share of the width. It stays in place while the page scrolls under it.
3. **The current tab** is the accent blue, and carries a blue bar on its top edge, so it never shows by colour alone.

- **Settings go to the user menu**, never to a sixth tab. The user menu is on every screen, one press away.
- **Every tab is big enough to hit**: at least 48 <Term id="dp">dp</Term> each way, as Android's accessibility guidance asks of every touch target. Apple's default size for a control is 44 points, and WCAG's smallest target, 24 pixels, is far below both.
- **The tab bar keeps clear of the phone's own bar** at the foot of the screen, where the system's gesture bar or buttons are.

### a screen opened from a view

A screen opened from a view, such as a film's page, keeps the tab bar. Its bar shows a back arrow and the screen's name, in place of the product's name, the search, the bell and the user menu, which wait on every view, one press away.

- **The tab bar stays**, so every view is still one press away. Apple's guidance asks the same, because without the tab bar people can forget where in the product they are. Only a screen that needs the whole screen, such as the player, covers it, and its own way out leads back.
- **Which tab is current depends on where the screen belongs.** A screen inside one view, such as one of the lists in the watchlist, keeps that view current. A screen that opens from several views, such as a film's page, which opens from home, from films, from the watchlist and from the search, is none of them, so no tab is current.
- **The back arrow** goes back to where people came from.
- **On a wide screen**, the same screen opens beside the same rail, under the same bar, and the same rule says which view is current.

## on a phone held sideways

A phone held sideways is wide but low, so the tabs move to the rail, and the frame gives the page the height.

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <SidewaysAnatomy />
  <figcaption>A phone held sideways, with films as the current view.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **The rail**, down the left edge: the tab bar's tabs, in the same order, each its icon alone.
2. **The current tab** is blue, with its bar on its left edge, as in the rail on a wide screen, without the lighter square: a phone has no pointer.
3. **The bar is slimmer**, and runs beside the rail rather than over it, with the same parts as when the phone is upright: the product's name, the search as an icon, the bell and the user menu.
4. **The page** takes the rest: the whole height under the bar.

It is the wide screen's rail, as wide, with the phone's tabs and nothing else. The product's name stays in the bar and settings stay in the user menu, as when the phone is upright, so turning the phone moves only the tabs. The rail and the bar keep clear of the camera's cutout and of the phone's own bar, on whichever side they are. A screen opened from a view shows its back arrow and its name in the slimmer bar, and the rail stays.

### a phone stays a phone

The frame follows the screen's short side, not its width. Turned sideways, a phone is as wide as a small tablet, but it is still a phone in people's hands, held the same way and used with the same thumbs. So a screen or a window less than 600 dp on its short side, 600 pixels in a browser, gets the phone's frame, held either way. With 600 or more, it gets the wide screen's frame: the rail under the top bar.

The line comes from Android's window sizes: a phone held upright is narrower than 600 dp, a phone held sideways is lower than 480, and most tablets are 600 or more across even when held upright.

| screen | its short side | frame |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A phone held upright | Under 600 | The tab bar at the foot |
| A phone held sideways | Under 600 | The rail at the left, and a slimmer bar |
| A tablet, or a window on a computer | 600 or more | The rail under the top bar, with the product's mark over it |
| A window on a computer, zoomed to 400% | Under 600: a 1280-pixel window becomes 320 | The phone's frame: the tab bar when the window is taller than wide, the rail when it is wider |
| A TV | Any | The TV's rail, because it is used with a remote, from across the room |

The frame is chosen by the screen. The components are not: they follow their own width, whatever the screen ([layout](/design-system/foundations/layout/#responsive-behaviour)).

## on a TV

A TV has no top bar. The rail holds everything the bar holds, one item under the other, so people reach all of it the same way: by pressing left.

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <TvClosed />
  <figcaption>A TV, with the rail closed while the focus is on the page.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **The avatar**, at the top: who is signed in. It opens the user menu.
2. **The search**, under it.
3. **The views**, in their order. While the focus is on the page, the rail shows their icons alone, and the current view keeps a blue bar on its left edge: the mark that [TV focus](/design-system/patterns/tv-focus/#one-meaning-for-the-ring) gives to where people came from.
4. **Notifications and settings**, at the foot, apart from the views. The bell shows its count, as in the top bar. As on a wide screen, settings have their place at the rail's foot, rather than in the user menu.
5. **The <Term id="safe-area">safe area</Term>.** Everything that can take focus, and all text, stays 48 dp from the left and right edges and 27 dp from the top and bottom, as on every [TV screen](/design-system/patterns/tv-focus/#movement-and-space). The rail's own surface may run to the edge.

Android's guidance for TVs puts them in the same places: the profile and the search at the top, settings at the foot. There is no product's name in the rail: home, its first view, takes people home.

### the rail opens

When the focus moves into the rail, the rail opens and shows each item's name beside its icon. On a <Term id="ten-foot">10-foot</Term> screen, icons alone ask too much: the rail holds more than a tab bar does, people see it from across the room, and they move the focus before they press, so the name has to say what the focused item opens.

<figure class="ds-figure">
  <TvOpen />
  <figcaption>The rail open, with the names. The focus has moved down to series, which has the ring; films, the current view, keeps its bar.</figcaption>
</figure>

- **<Kbd>←</Kbd> from the page's first column** moves the focus into the rail and opens it, with the focus on the current view.
- **<Kbd>↑</Kbd> and <Kbd>↓</Kbd> move** between the items. Moving opens nothing, so passing a view on the way to settings doesn't load it.
- **<Kbd>ok</Kbd> opens** the focused item, closes the rail, and puts the focus on the page it opened.
- **<Kbd>→</Kbd> or <Kbd>back</Kbd> closes the rail** and puts the focus back on the page, where it was.
- **The focused item** has the TV focus of a row: the ring inside its edge, the lighter surface, a little growth and brighter text. The ring always means focus, and only focus; the current view keeps its bar, so the two never look alike.
- **The rail opens at once**, over the page, without sliding, and the page stays where it is. Only the ring and the growth settle, in 150 ms, as on every TV screen.

## how it behaves

- **The frame is the same on every view.** Moving between views changes the page, and nothing else.
- **It stays on every screen opened from a view**, except one that needs the whole screen, such as the player.
- **A view keeps its place** while people are elsewhere: back on it, they find it scrolled where they left it. Pressing the current view again goes back to the start of it, at the top.
- **The current mark moves at once.** The blue, the square and the bars change in place, without sliding from one view to the next, as the current tab's underline does; see [motion](/design-system/styles/motion/#what-doesnt-move).
- **The tooltip is the browser's own.** It shows after the browser's short delay, only under the pointer, and goes when the pointer leaves.
- **Turning the phone keeps people's place**, and so does opening and closing the rail on a TV.

## accessibility

Planned, together with the components:

- **Landmarks.** The top bar is the page's header <Term id="landmark">landmark</Term>, as on every screen. The views are a navigation landmark of their own, named “views”, wherever they are: in the rail or in the tab bar. The page is the main landmark.
- **Each view is a link**, named after its view: “films”. The name is text that only <Term id="assistive-technology">assistive technology</Term> reads, and the icon is decoration, hidden from it, as in [iconography](/design-system/styles/iconography/#decoration-and-meaning). On a wide screen, the same word is the link's tooltip, and assistive technology may read it a second time, as a description, as with an icon button.
- **The product's mark is a link** named after the product and where it goes, as the top bar's name is: the product's name and “home”. The chevron and the letter are decoration.
- **The current view is told, not only drawn.** A screen reader hears “films, current page” on the view itself. On a screen inside one view, that view is marked as current; on a screen that is none of the views, none is.
- **The focus follows the screen.** On a wide screen, the top bar comes first, the mark, the search, the bell and the user menu, then the rail, then the page. On a phone held upright, the tab bar comes last, after the page, where it sits; held sideways, the rail comes after the bar and before the page. Turning the phone keeps the focus where it was.
- **The rail and the tab bar are one tab stop each**, as a toolbar is ([interaction](/design-system/foundations/interaction/#the-keyboard-model)). <Kbd>tab</Kbd> moves onto the current view, or onto the first one when none is current, and the arrow keys move along: up and down in the rail, left and right in the tab bar, with <Kbd>home</Kbd> and <Kbd>end</Kbd> for the first and the last. <Kbd>enter</Kbd> opens the view. Settings, at the rail's foot, is part of the same stop.
- **Contrast.** The muted grey of the icons at rest, 9.4:1 on the rail, and the blue of the current one, 6.8:1 on its lighter square, are tested at 4.5:1 on every background, like text, well above the 3:1 an icon that carries meaning needs (WCAG 1.4.11); see [colour](/design-system/styles/colour/#contrast). The lighter square is only 1.1:1 against the rail, so it never marks the current view on its own: the blue and the bar do.
- **On a TV**, each item in the rail is read with its name, open or closed, and the current view is read as current. The focus looks the way [TV focus](/design-system/patterns/tv-focus/) has it, and with animations switched off on the device, the ring and the growth appear at once.

## the words

- **The views' names.** One word each, a noun, lowercase, as people name the place: “home”, “films”, “series”, “watchlist”, “downloads”. The same word as a view's tooltip, as its name for a screen reader, in the TV's rail and as the view's title.
- **The product's mark.** The first letter of the product's name, as the name is written in the bar: lowercase. Its tooltip, and its name for a screen reader, are the product's name and “home”.
- **No “more”, no “menu”.** Every tab is a place; there is no tab for the rest.
- **The back arrow** is named after where it goes: “back to films”.
- **The screen's name in the bar** is the screen's own title, with a name's own capitals: “The Quiet Harbour”.
- **The rail on a TV.** The person's name beside the avatar, then “search”, the views, “notifications” and “settings”.
- **The landmark.** Named “views”, after what it holds, as the top bar of these pages names its “sections”.

- **The icons' size.** 24 is the icon set's own grid, so the 1.75 line is 1.75 pixels, and the size of the icons in Android's navigation rail and navigation bar, and in Android TV's navigation drawer. It is one step past the largest size that [iconography](/design-system/styles/iconography/#sizes) lists today, 22, a tile's app icon.
- **In code.** The views are links in a `<nav aria-label="views">`: the rail, or the tab bar. The current view's link has `aria-current="page"`, and on a screen inside one view, that view's link has `aria-current="true"`, as the top bar of these pages marks its current section. A view's name is visually hidden text inside its link, the same word is the link's `title`, and its icon is `aria-hidden`. The current view's link has `tabindex="0"`, or the first one's when none is current, the others `-1`, and the arrow keys move the focus between them. The mark is a link to home, with the product's name and “home” as its name and its `title`.
- **The phone's own bars.** The tab bar and the rail leave room for the system's bars and the camera's cutout: on the web, with `viewport-fit=cover`, they add `env(safe-area-inset-bottom)`, `env(safe-area-inset-left)` and `env(safe-area-inset-right)` to their padding.
- **Sources.** Android: the navigation bar (three to five destinations), window size classes, touch target size (48 dp), the navigation rail and the navigation bar of Compose Material 3 (`NavigationRail` at least 80 dp wide, from `NavigationRailCollapsedTokens.NarrowContainerWidth`; icons of 24 dp, `NavigationRailBaselineItemTokens.IconSize` and `NavigationBarVerticalItemTokens.IconSize`), and the TV navigation drawer (collapsed to icons, open with their names; five or six destinations; icons of 24 dp, `NavigationDrawerItemDefaults.IconSize`). Apple's Human Interface Guidelines: tab bars (labels, overflow, keep the tab bar in view) and accessibility (control sizes). WCAG 2.2: 1.4.1 use of colour, 1.4.11 non-text contrast, 2.5.8 target size. This system: the button's hover and focus, the icon button's label as its name and tooltip, the brand's chevron and wordmark, the token contrast test. And the rail as a product for watching films builds it on the web today: 80 px wide, its icons 24 in squares of 48, 8 apart, settings at its foot, the current view blue on a lighter square.
